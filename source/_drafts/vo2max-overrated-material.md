# VO2max is the wrong number to chase — article material

Working title ideas: *"The VO2max delusion"* / *"Your watch's favourite useless number"* / *"Stop racing your VO2max."*

**Tenet (the thesis to defend):** VO2max has become the hot, universal yardstick runners brag and compete with — but it's a poor one: the number your watch gives you is of debatable accuracy, and *even if it were accurate*, it isn't an actionable metric for running training.

Scope: endurance running (and by extension cycling/other endurance). Not a health/longevity piece, though the longevity hype is part of *why* the number got hot — see §1.

---

## 1. The hype: how VO2max is touted (and pushed by watch makers)

### Watch makers put it front and centre

- **Garmin (via Firstbeat).** Garmin bakes an automatic VO2max estimate into essentially every running watch (Firstbeat Analytics, from HR-vs-pace during runs), surfaces it as a headline "fitness" number, and layers derived features on top of it (Training Status, Race Predictor, "Fitness Age"). Garmin is among the leading global smartwatch makers, so this is the number millions see after every run. (Firstbeat/Garmin; Statista Market Insights 2024.)
- **Polar** markets it hardest rhetorically: a guide literally titled **"VO2max: The Ultimate Training Blueprint,"** calling it *"the 'gold standard' of cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) and a powerful predictor of long-term metabolic health,"* and placing users on a **colour-coded scale toward 'Elite.'** <https://www.polar.com/us-en/guide/what-is-a-good-vo2max>
- **Apple Watch** has shipped a VO2max ("Cardio Fitness") estimate since watchOS 4 (2017), updated in 2021, shown in the Health app with **notifications warning of "Low" cardio fitness** — i.e. Apple actively nudges hundreds of millions of wrists about this number. (PLOS One 2025 validation study; Apple.)
- **Coros, Fitbit, Garmin, Suunto, plus Whoop/Oura** all now compute a VO2max or CRF estimate — it's become a default, expected wearable stat.

### The longevity influencers made it a moral metric

- **Peter Attia** popularised the framing that **"VO2max is the single greatest predictor of your lifespan"** (a literal YouTube title: *"Why VO2 max is the greatest predictor of lifespan"*; central to *Outlive*). This spilled from longevity culture into running culture.
- Pushback exists and is worth citing: cardiologist **Eric Topol** calls Attia's "single greatest predictor" claim **false**, notes wearable VO2max has MAPE ~7–16% and is being over-emphasised (now amplified by AI reading watch data), and argues METs are better-evidenced for health outcomes. <https://erictopol.substack.com/p/the-flawed-v02-max-craze>
- A longevity-research explainer agrees the "single greatest predictor" line is **"stronger than the evidence supports"** — most evidence is observational, and a 2025 Mendelian-randomisation study did **not** support a simple causal effect of (estimated) VO2max on lifespan. <https://freegym.ai/10x-longevity/articles/exercise-physiology/cardiorespiratory-fitness/is-vo2-max-the-best-predictor-of-longevity>

### It became social currency / bragging rights

- *"In the endurance world, VO2 Max is the ultimate 'bragging rights' number. You'll hear it thrown around on club runs and Strava comments like a badge of honour."* — Total Endurance. <https://www.total-endurance.co.uk/blog/what-is-vo2-max>
- *"The number glows with authority… The metric has become the measure of the runner."* — Cult of Running, "The VO2 Max Myth." <https://cultofrunning.com/writings/articles/the-vo2-max-myth>
- Trainer Sebastian Katz (in Yahoo/《The VO2 Max Trap》): for most healthy non-elites *"the idea that VO2 max adds anything to any part of your life is completely insane,"* and the wearable number *"is bullshit… like ChatGPT — sometimes it's right, sometimes it's not."* <https://health.yahoo.com/wellness/fitness/exercise/articles/vo2-max-trap-trendy-wont-175203951.html>

**Takeaway for the piece:** the hype has three engines — (1) wearables that compute and gamify it by default, (2) longevity influencers who moralised it, (3) social platforms that turned it into a comparable score. None of those three engines is *running-performance* science.

## 2. Fact-check of the thesis

### Claim A — "VO2max is now super hot and used as a universal measure to compete with."
**Verdict: TRUE / well-supported.** See §1. Ubiquitous on wearables, elevated by longevity media, traded as bragging rights on Strava/club runs. This is a cultural claim and the evidence for it is abundant. No correction needed.

### Claim B — "The accuracy of a watch VO2max is debatable."
**Verdict: TRUE / well-supported — with a fair nuance.**

Wearable VO2max is **not a measurement; it's a regression estimate** of what a lab test would say. Reported error:

- **Garmin Forerunner 245** vs lab (**Engel, Masur, Sperlich & Düking, 2026**, *Eur J Appl Physiol* 126, 591–603): overall **MAPE 7.2–7.9%**; but strongly fitness-dependent — **highly-trained MAPE 9.4–10.4%** (watch *underestimated* by ~6.3 ml/kg/min; poor reliability, ICC 0.34–0.41), moderately-trained MAPE 2.8–4.1%. Conclusion: "valid in moderately trained… less valid in highly trained… caution warranted." <https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00421-025-05923-x> · plain-English roast: <https://the5krunner.com/2025/08/27/garmin-vo2max-scientific-study-roasts-it-and-loves-it-when-a-it-dpends/>
- **Earlier FR245 study** (Düking et al., 2022, PMC9286863): overall **MAPE 5.7%**, but it **underestimated the fittest band (55–61 ml/kg/min) by ~6.2%** — same fit-runner bias — and the authors explicitly discuss the (limited) usefulness for guiding training. Good dual-purpose cite for B **and** C. <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9286863/>
- **Apple Watch Series 7** (Caserman et al. 2024): **MAPE ~15.8%**; PLOS One (2025) found the Apple Watch **underestimates** VO2max in fit people. <https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0323741>
- **Polar V800** (Passler et al. 2019): **MAPE ~13.2%**; Fitbit ~10%.
- **Systematic review** (Frontiers, 2025): Garmin/Firstbeat generally **MAPE <10%**; accuracy varies by device and protocol. <https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2025.1707991/full>
- **Topol:** across Apple/Garmin/Fitbit, MAPE **7–16%**; devices **underestimate the fit, overestimate the unfit**; sensitive to optical-HR error, device fit, hydration, altitude, ambient temperature; sometimes derived from as little as a 6-minute walk.

**The fair nuance (include it, or a critic will):** several validation papers conclude the estimate is *"acceptable for tracking trends over time"* (MAPE <10%, CCC >0.7) even if it's poor for a precise individual value. And accuracy is *worst exactly where the bragging happens* — among highly-trained runners (systematic ~5–6 point underestimation). So the honest phrasing isn't "it's random noise," it's: **fine as a rough personal trend line; not precise enough to rank yourself against other runners, and systematically biased for the fit.** That actually strengthens the "useless for competing" angle while staying accurate.

### Claim C — "Even when accurate, VO2max is not an actionable metric in running training."
**Verdict: MOSTLY TRUE — needs one precision tweak.**

Supporting evidence:

- **It's a ceiling, not a dial.** VO2max describes the *size* of the aerobic engine; it doesn't tell you what workout to do tomorrow. You program training from **paces/zones, lactate threshold, and RPE** — not from "my number is 54." The watch number changing from 52→54 implies no training action.
- **It plateaus in trained runners.** VO2max rises fast in beginners (which is why apps love it — it moves and is easy to show), then plateaus; further performance gains come from **threshold, economy, mitochondrial density, durability, pacing.** (E3Coach; Cult of Running.) *"Your engine may not get much bigger… but you can become dramatically better at using it."*
- **Performance separation isn't VO2max.** Among reasonably fit runners, VO2max values **cluster**; the separation is **lactate threshold and running economy** (classic Conley & Krahenbuhl; Bassett & Howley 2000 — LT is the single strongest predictor of distance performance). In homogeneous well-trained groups, VO2max **loses discriminative power**; for 1500 m the best predictor is **vVO2max**, and for 5 k–marathon it's the **blood-lactate response.** (PMC9253837.) <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9253837/>
- **Elites set PBs while VO2max declines.** A recurring, citable point: athletes run their fastest times as measured VO2max falls, improving economy/threshold instead. (Cult of Running; multiple coaches.)

**Framing note:** keep the whole piece about **the VO2max number** — that's the only thing the hype is about, and it's the only thing being claimed non-actionable. (No need to detour into vVO2max/threshold paces to "defend" the point — nobody is conflating the score with training paces, so raising it just sounds defensive.) The one small, honest concession: the number *is* mildly useful **as a rough, long-term personal trend** (is my base fitness drifting up over months?) — a low bar, and not "competing," so it doesn't threaten the thesis.

- **The big honest counter-source to preempt:** the 2026 *Sports Medicine* re-analysis of Joyner's model across **888 people** found VO2max was the **strongest single predictor** of lab performance proxies (65–76% of speed at LT/LTP), economy second (20–24%). **But** read the caveats the authors themselves give: (a) that's across a **heterogeneous** recreational-to-world-class range (VO2max always wins when you compare a couch potato to an Olympian); (b) within **homogeneous** trained groups its discriminative power collapses; (c) the study **explicitly does not show causation** — raising VO2max doesn't guarantee faster racing. So it supports "VO2max correlates with fitness across a wide range," **not** "your VO2max is the number to train by." <https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-026-02439-y>

## 3. Net verdict on the thesis

The thesis is **sound and defensible**, with two edits that make it sharper and harder to attack:

1. **Reframe B from "inaccurate" to "imprecise and biased where it matters."** It's a decent personal trend line but not a cross-runner ranking tool, and it systematically *understates* fit runners — so using it to compete is doubly wrong.
2. **Keep C precise: "the VO2max *number* is not a training input."** The actionable levers (threshold, economy, durability, paces) live *under* the ceiling. No need to argue against vVO2max/intervals — nobody's confusing the wrist score with a training pace.

One-line version that survives fact-check:

> The watch's VO2max is a gamified estimate of your aerobic *ceiling* — imprecise, biased against the fit, and not a training input. Runners improve by raising their **threshold**, **economy**, and **durability**, and they race on **paces**, not on a two-digit score. Chasing the number optimises the wrong thing.

## 3b. Additional aspects (fact-checked)

**(i) VO2max is largely genetic — TRUE, both baseline and trainability.**
HERITAGE Family Study (Bouchard et al., 1999): 481 people, identical 20-wk program; VO2max *response* ~**47% heritable**, 2.5× more variance between families than within; individual gains ranged **−114 to +1097 ml/min** (non-responders → super-responders). Baseline VO2max also strongly familial. Genomic follow-up (Bouchard 2011): a 21-SNP panel explains ~49% of trainability. → Use to argue "a big chunk of the number is inherited."

**(ii) The max-HR analogy — SOUND (with the user's own "not quite as bad" caveat).**
Max HR is a largely fixed, individual/genetic trait: drifts down with age, minimally trainable, and **uncorrelated with fitness/performance** (a higher max HR ≠ faster). VO2max shares the "comparing genetics, not ability" flaw — but is *less* bad because (a) it's meaningfully trainable and (b) it does track fitness across a wide range. Good rhetorical framing for the genetics point.

**(iii) Similar VO2max → different results, and similar times → different VO2max — TRUE (both directions).**
- Conley & Krahenbuhl (1980), elite 10k, similar VO2max (~71.7): VO2max↔time **r = −0.12 (n.s.)**; running economy explained **65.4%** of race-time variance. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7453514/>
- Reverse: "great variations in VO2max among runners with similar performance" (Sjödin & Svedenhag 1985, *Sports Med*, PMID 3890068); Costill found two 2:18 marathoners with **50% vs 98%** slow-twitch fibres. Economy can differ ~40% between elite and untrained.

**(iv) VO2max matters less in running than XC skiing/cycling — TRUE, frame carefully.**
- Highest recorded values rank **XC skiing > running > cycling**, driven by **muscle mass recruited / test modality** (Williams; Seiler), *not* by "importance." (Records: Svendsen 97.5, Dæhlie 96 — Scandinavian/whole-body.) Don't lean on this ordering to mean "importance."
- The *running-specific* point that actually supports the claim: running has **large running-economy variance**, so VO2max is a weak discriminator among runners; where efficiency varies less (e.g. cycling gross efficiency sits in a narrow band), aerobic capacity tracks performance more tightly. So obsessing over VO2max makes *least* sense in running.

**(v) Kläbo quote — UNVERIFIED. Norwegian lactate method — TRUE.**
- Could **not** find a citable source for Kläbo saying he ignores VO2max as non-actionable. Closest verifiable: NIH (Norges idrettshøgskole) analysis that he "doesn't necessarily need the field's highest VO2maks to be best." <https://www.nih.no/om/aktuelt/bloggen/2026/hvordan-kan-klaebo-vere-best-pa-bade-sprint-og-femm.html> → **don't publish the quote as fact without a source.**
- Norwegian Method (lactate-guided threshold; Ingebrigtsen double-threshold at 2–3 mmol/l): well documented. Bakken <https://www.mariusbakken.com/the-norwegian-model.html>; Outside on the Ingebrigtsens.
