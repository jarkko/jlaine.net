# Fieldstones

Raw material for future content. Stories, observations, anecdotes, vignettes from coaching practice. Following Gerald Weinberg's fieldstone method (*Weinberg on Writing: The Fieldstone Method*, 2005).

## The metaphor

New England farmers built walls from stones they unearthed while plowing. They gathered stones in heaps as the fields produced them, and only later assembled walls from the pile. Stones came from doing the work, not from going hunting for stones.

The writing parallel:

- Gather "stones" (ideas, anecdotes, observations) as they surface in daily life
- Don't filter, don't worry yet about how they'll be used
- Later, browse the pile, find stones that fit, assemble into a wall (post, chapter, essay)
- The collection is the persistent asset; individual writings are derivable from it

Capture comes from doing the coaching, not from sitting down to "find content." Connection happens later.

## Format

Each stone goes here as it occurs. Append to the end. Each stone needs: a date and the stone itself. Tags in brackets optional but useful for later browsing.

Example:

> ### 2026-05-26
> At U14 girls practice, I yelled "go to the net!" and one of them stepped *back* instead of forward. Two practices later, with the +2 net-touch bonus in place and zero verbal direction from me, she went up on her own. [tags: blocking, u14, attractor, instruction-paradox]

Guidelines:

- **Concrete.** Specific moment, specific observation. A name (or "one of the U14s"), a place, a sentence said, a movement seen.
- **Don't generalize at capture time.** Generalization is part of wall-building, not stone-gathering. If a stone wants to be a lesson, let it. If it wants to stay a story, leave it as one.
- **Either language.** Write in whatever language the moment surfaced in. Mixing Finnish and English here is fine — this is the dump, not the published wall.
- **Naming people.** Real names are fine in stones (helps you recall context later). If a person shouldn't appear in any published version, flag the heading with `[DO NOT NAME IN PUBLIC]` and add tag `do-not-name`. Anonymization happens at wall-building, not at capture.
- **Trust the pile.** A stone that looks small or uninteresting now might be the key piece of a wall in six months.

### 2026-05-26 [theme]

In 2v2 beach (youth + recreational adult), the default is for both players to stay back after their own serve rather than have one at the net for blocking. We want them playing the "real" beach game where one blocks (even if they peel off after the set) and the other defends. Explicit instruction doesn't stick — they revert when the rule disappears. Even at the advanced level, the pattern works after a serve but collapses in transition, especially without a dedicated blocker — *role ambiguity under time pressure* defaults the system to the safer "both back."

This is the seed observation for the block-defense post arc. ### 2026-05-28 [insight]

Why staying back "feels right" to the kids even when it's costing them: any *single* rally often doesn't punish both-back. The weak hitter mishits, the loose set goes nowhere, the opponents make their own error. So the kids' local picture — *this* rally, *this* hitter, *this* set — tells them staying back was fine. They're solving the visible problem correctly.

What the local picture hides is the aggregate:

- Overpasses with nobody up there to put away
- Sets that drift over the net and need to be handled there
- Sets so tight a low block beats no block
- The presence effect — just having a body up changes the hitter's swing, the setter's set, the comfort of the whole opposing system, with no touch required

Over enough rallies, the un-blocked hitter has freedom that gradually wins. But the kid can't see that pattern in any single moment. They feel safe rally-by-rally and stay deep.

This is precisely what a well-designed constraint does: it makes the aggregate cost local. A scoring rule that doubles points for net-side touches converts an invisible long-run truth into a visible per-rally consequence. The kid doesn't have to track patterns across a session in their head — the game does it for them, rally by rally.

This generalises beyond block/defense. Many "stuck" coaching problems are aggregate-cost problems that the player's local picture hides. The pedagogical move is the same: design a constraint that makes the aggregate cost local. [tags: insight, pedagogy, constraints, aggregation, invisible-cost, both-back, beach, scoring-design, generalisable]

### 2026-06-10 [insight]

The game is messy, so training should be allowed to be messy too. If practice looks clean, orderly, and coach-controlled all the time, it may be because the game has been simplified past recognition. Real play has incomplete information, bad timing, awkward contacts, ambiguous roles, pressure, hesitation, and recovery from mistakes.

Messy practice is not something to be ashamed of. It can be a sign that the players are working inside the real problem instead of performing a cleaned-up imitation of it. The coaching question is not "does this look tidy?" but "is this mess representative of the game, and are the players learning to solve it?" [tags: insight, representative-learning, messiness, constraints, game-based-learning, coaching-shame, practice-design]

  - Quote: nonlinear pedagogy captures "movement variability, self-organization, emergent decision making, and symmetry-breaking" as consequences of interacting constraints.

The defensible claim is "mess that preserves the real information, decisions, and constraints of the game is legitimate and often necessary," not "all messy practice is good."

### 2026-06-10 [book]

*Transforming Basketball: Changing How We Think About Basketball Performance* (Alex Sarama, 2024). Sarama (Cleveland Cavaliers player development → Portland Fire head coach) applies ecological dynamics, nonlinear pedagogy, and the constraints-led approach to basketball. Core stance: skill is not a stored technique recalled on demand but something that emerges in the player–task–environment interaction; therefore practice must keep perception and action coupled, and the coach's job shifts from instructor of "correct" movements to designer of environments. Ideas worth translating to volleyball / orienteering:

1. **"Repetition without repetition"** (Bernstein via Sarama): "Players are still getting reps, but every repetition is going to be slightly different... players don't learn by sheer repetition, but they learn from differences in repetition." Lowest-hanging fruit in basketball: never shoot twice from the same spot. Volleyball: never serve twice from the same place to the same target; vary the toss, distance, and ball in reception lines instead of grooving identical free balls. Orienteering: never run a control-picking leg the same way twice; vary map scale, direction of attack, speed.
2. **Constrain the opponent instead of removing them.** When the live game is too hard for a beginner, the traditional move is to delete the opponent (hit against no block, shoot against air). Sarama instead degrades the opponent: "We're going to constrain the defense so that the defense cannot jump. Or maybe they can only play holding a basketball." The learner keeps the real perceptual problem but gets success reps. Volleyball: blocker holds a ball, blocker may not jump, defender starts seated — instead of taking the block away from young hitters.
3. **"It's not just having the opponent, it's having the constraints. That's the real magic sauce."** Small-sided games alone are not the method; *shaping* them is. Direct echo of our verkkokosketus tuplaa / painealue work — playing 2v2 and hoping is not the same as designing the 2v2.
4. **Principles of play over set plays** — knowing *how* to play instead of *what* to play. Sarama's example set ("play in zero seconds" when you have an advantage, "prevent one defender guarding two," "escape the lava") is basketball-specific, but the move — a small set of always-on principles instead of memorized patterns — transfers. Volleyball: transition principles instead of rehearsed combination plays. Orienteering: route-choice principles instead of memorized procedures per terrain type.
5. **Player development must be integrated with how the team plays.** "What we often see... is that player development is completely different from how the team plays... we're really just wasting time or just leaving player development up to chance." Individual technique sessions divorced from the team's game model are the volleyball norm too.
6. **Organized practice must replace lost free play.** "Playing outside, playing in the park is being replaced with skills trainers and AAU... it's even more important that we replicate these environments in practice because players these days are simply not growing up and actually accessing these opportunities." Finnish parallel: kids no longer play pihapeli volleyball or roam forests; practice has to supply the variability that the backyard and the metsä used to.
7. **Differential learning for stuck/struggling skills**: deliberately maximal variability without an opponent (1v0) as a reset tool for athletes whose technique has plateaued — not as the default method, as a targeted intervention.

### 2026-06-10 [insight]

In football there's the idea that you learn to *tame the ball* — before dribbling, passing, or shooting, the child builds a comfortable, confident relationship with the ball through playful exploration: rolling it, tapping it with every surface, stopping it, losing it, getting it back. The same is very much true in volleyball. Yet we're quick to point out technical flaws early on, and run lots of technique drills, instead of stuff that teaches kids to play around with the ball and tame it in whichever way possible — bumping it against a wall, juggling it with platform/hands/head, catching weird balls, keeping it alive any way they can. The taming repertoire doesn't have to be conventional at all: dribbling, peppering or playing pokey-only, underhand touches, underhand spike-style slaps, always spiking under the ball to create backspin — whatever keeps the kid manipulating the ball and discovering how it responds.

The flaw-hunting reflex assumes there is one correct technique that must be installed before the ball relationship can develop. The football tradition assumes the opposite order: relationship with the ball first, refinement later. A kid who has tamed the ball has thousands of varied touches and a body that already knows how the ball behaves; technique work then shapes something that exists. A kid drilled on "correct" forearm-pass form from day one has a posture and a fear, but no relationship. [tags: insight, ball-taming, ball-familiarity, football-transfer, technique-drills, flaw-hunting, implicit-learning, deliberate-play, youth, practice-design]

Deliberate play "is informally regulated by modified rules that are established, enforced and further modified by the participants themselves" — i.e. taming the ball is what deliberate play does, and the Developmental Model of Sport Participation argues early deliberate play before deliberate practice benefits talent development.

  - Transforming Basketball, "Ecological Dynamics: The Core Principles Behind The CLA": https://transformingbball.com/ecological-dynamics-the-core-principles-behind-the-cla/ — Quote: "Unlike traditional models that promote the coach as the master instructor and a one-size-fits-all approach to techniques, ecological dynamics recognizes that different movement solutions can be equally effective depending on the individual and situation."

Pushback / caveat: the science supports *delaying rule-heavy explicit correction* and *maximizing playful, varied ball contact* — it does not support never teaching technique. Implicit-learning findings in children are promising but thinner than in adults (Capio et al. note "little evidence derived from children"), and Kal et al. (2018) conclude the automatization advantage of implicit learning has "some evidence" but needs stronger studies. Volleyball differs from football in that the ball may not rest — no catching, no carrying in the real game — but this is a design detail, not a real obstacle: a wall, a floor (dribbling), or a friend is enough, and the unconventional repertoire above works solo or in pairs just fine. And some early technical scaffolding (e.g. safe overhead-passing hand shape) plausibly prevents injury and frustration; the defensible claim is "relationship before refinement," not "no refinement."

### 2026-06-26 [insight]

"Skill" is a trap word, because there are **two different technique/skill distinctions** floating around and they contradict each other. Coaches who think they agree often don't.

- **The coaching-tradition split:** *technique* is the movement; *skill* is "the right technique at the right time" — i.e. **skill = ability + technique** (brianmac, Soccer Coach Weekly, the Knapp 1963 lineage). It feels obviously true. But it's quietly information-processing: it assumes you *perceive → pick the technique → execute*, with the executing built first and the deciding bolted on top. This is exactly the frame under the Finnish federation's *lajilinjaukset* (Opetusavaimet + "simple is repeatable," with "read the game" added afterward) and under both basketball models' decision layers.
- **The ecological split:** *skill* is the **adaptive fit between player and game** — using the information in front of you to find and carry out a solution to *this* rally. *Technique* is just **one** coordination solution, not a foundation to install first and not separable from the perceiving/deciding. Rob Gray's line (the one Sarama quotes): "skill is the ability of a player to use information from the environment to find and execute a movement solution." Araújo & Davids: skill acquisition isn't acquiring an entity, it's "the emergence of an adaptive, functional relationship between an organism and its environment" — better called *skill attunement*. Bernstein got there first: dexterity is "the ability to create a perfect key for any emerging lock."

The practical bite: you can't say you've "gone ecological" and still define skill as applying the right technique at the right time — that re-imports the very split you claim to have dropped. The honest middle: technique work isn't banned (an action is never wholly new; bounded isolated reps can widen the repertoire), but treating one technique as *the* answer, or as a *prerequisite to playing*, is the "organismic asymmetry" — over-investing in the athlete and starving the task/environment. Iida on the ground vs. in the air (the attractor stone above) is this in one body: she *has* the technique; what she lacks under in-air timing is the *skill* — the functional solution emerging under the real constraints. [tags: insight, technique-vs-skill, skill-definition, ecological-dynamics, information-processing, organismic-asymmetry, bernstein, gray, araujo-davids, model-source]

Possible references and quotes to check:

- Newell, K. M. (1985), *Coordination, Control and Skill* — coordination → control → skill; learning as search in the perceptual-motor workspace.
- Araújo, D. & Davids, K. (2011), "What exactly is acquired during skill acquisition?", *J. Consciousness Studies* 18(3–4) — organismic asymmetry; "skill adaptation/attunement."
- Gray, R. — *How We Learn to Move* / *Perception & Action* podcast: skill as using information to find and execute a movement solution; *action capacity* vs *skill*.
- Woods, Rudd, Robertson & Davids (2021), "Enskilment," *Sports Medicine – Open*, PMC: skill as progressive attunement / education of attention.

Pushback / caveat: the eco-D definition is well published, but the comparison shouldn't be a strawman — the coaching-tradition split is internally coherent and many great coaches use it productively. The claim here isn't "tradition is wrong about everything," it's "the two definitions rest on different theories of how skill forms, so a model has to pick one and stay consistent (we pick the ecological one, per model §4/§7)." Also: skilledathleticism.com and others on the eco-D side explicitly defend a bounded role for technique work — don't overclaim that technique is useless.
