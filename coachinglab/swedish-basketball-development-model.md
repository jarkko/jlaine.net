# The Swedish Basketball Development Model — Brief

### Ecological dynamics & the constraints-led approach in a national framework

A case study featured in Alex Sarama's *Transforming Basketball*.

---

## What it is

The Swedish Basketball Federation (Svenska Basketbollförbundet, SBBF) published its **Framework for Player Development** (*Ramverk för spelarutveckling*) in **January 2020**, after just over two years of reviewing research and literature, interviewing experienced Swedish and international coaches, external monitoring, consultations, and a large number of drafts.

- Covers all levels of player development, ages **U8–U19**.
- Explicitly **evidence-informed**, grounded in contemporary sports science — but framed as a recommendation, not a mandate. A coach, team, or club decides how far to implement it: as a whole, in parts, or not at all.
- Underpins the federation's educational materials and modules, competition regulations, and development environments.

## Why it matters / why the book features it

SBBF is one of the **first national basketball federations in the world** to build its player-development and coach-education systems on **ecological dynamics, the constraints-led approach (CLA), and nonlinear pedagogy**.

- **Consulting:** Alex Sarama (through Basketball Immersion / Transforming Basketball; later Director of Player Development with the NBA's Cleveland Cavaliers) supported the framework with coaching clinics across Sweden, produced Swedish coach-education videos on CLA and conceptual offense, and designed the Jr. NBA / NBA Basketball Schools curriculum used worldwide.
- **Architects:** The federation's own coach educators **Andrew Pleick** and **Rikard Aspegren** drive the framework and coach education. They hosted Mark O'Sullivan on their Swedish coaching podcast.
- **Wider movement:** Per Transforming Basketball, the **Norwegian, Finnish, and Icelandic** federations (a clear Scandinavian cluster) plus the regional federations of **Bavaria and Berlin** in Germany have also adopted an ecological approach.

---

## The four pillars

1. **See the whole human being** — the person playing basketball, not just the athlete.
2. **Development is non-linear** — each individual is unique; development is not linear or predictable. Biological maturity can differ by as much as **five years** between two players born in the same year.
3. **Self-determination theory** — motivation rests on three needs: **autonomy** (freedom of choice), **competence** (effectively meeting your environment), and **relatedness** (others care for me). (Ryan & Deci, 2000.)
4. **Player development is based on the game (the match)** — the match is the reference point for everything taught.

---

## The five golden principles

These are the design rules for creating representative practice — i.e. high task representativeness so learning transfers to the game.

1. **Many and varied repetitions** — lots of reps are needed, but because game situations are never the same (free throws aside), reps must be **varied** (different starting points each time).
2. **Lots of decisions** — face players with the decisions they'd meet in a 5v5 game. Young/inexperienced players can handle only a few; scale the number up with maturity and experience (e.g. include both an offensive and a defensive player so a real decision exists).
3. **Game-like** — practice must resemble what players will see in a game, **representative of their age and context** (for a 10-year-old that's the district U11 league; for a pro it's EuroLeague).
4. **Effective execution** — judge a skill by the **solution / outcome**, not by conformity to one fixed technical template. There is no "one-size-fits-all" technique; different bodies find different functional solutions.
5. **Let the players reflect** — giving the answer has a quick, superficial effect; letting players reflect and discover it themselves has a deeper, lasting effect. Use questions: *"What did you notice? What did it look like? What worked well?"*

---

## How skill learning is modelled

- **Complex dynamical systems.** Both the individual player and the player's context are treated as **non-linear dynamical systems**.
- **"Loadings" = constraints (laddningar).** Built on **Newell's (1986)** model of three interacting constraints — individual, task, environment. The coaching moves:
  - Manipulate the task to focus on what you want to develop in the players.
  - Give feedback largely **directly from the game** itself.
  - **Preserve the complexity of the match and make the task *easier* — don't break it apart** (no decomposing into on-air dribbling or cone/fake-defender drills; "keep it all together").
- **Steering tools:** different **scoring** rules guide behaviour (e.g. double points for a left-hand finish); **differentiated loads** let one exercise serve players of very different maturity in the same session.
- **Theory stack also cites:** Côté's developmental model (deliberate play / sampling), the Athletic Talent Development Environment (ATDE), and Lloyd & Oliver's Youth Physical Development model for the physical side.

---

## The game model

- Basketball's core idea: **score by creating advantages**, and prevent the opponent from scoring by reducing/denying *their* advantages. This logic, plus the complexity of a real match, guides which skills a player needs.
- **Principles of play / conceptual offense over set plays.** Players who understand the aims of each phase can play *both* structured set plays *and* free-flowing, principle-based offense. Introducing set plays too early risks players who can run the system but can't read the game.
- **Up to and including U16: no predetermined decisions** — players must always be free to break the pattern, so they learn to scan, interpret, find solutions, and decide.
- **Coach behaviour:** call out instructions only sparingly during games; the aim is to give players the tools to interpret the court themselves. Reinforce brave decisions made outside the comfort zone. Help players **self-evaluate** rather than instructing them what to do.

## Decision-making (BESLUTSFATTANDE) — and a theoretical wrinkle

The framework models decision-making as **four overlapping components**, drawn as a Venn diagram:

- **SKANNA** (scan) — use the senses to scan the court; the ability to perceive information grows with biological maturation and brain development, and experience improves directing attention to the most relevant information.
- **TOLKA** (interpret) — learn to interpret/understand information (angles, distances, spaces, opponents' positions and actions). Varied, game-like practice builds up an **"erfarenhetsbank" (experience bank)** of situations.
- **BESLUTA** (decide) — choosing well is complex; beyond correctly-interpreted information it's affected by self-confidence, status in the group, and the team's game-plan.
- **AKTION** (action) — the actual on-court act (a dribble, a ball-screen, or an off-ball move to create space).

**Worth flagging:** this scan → interpret → decide → act chain, with an internal "experience bank," is an **information-processing / cognitivist** model of skill — the classic perception → cognition → action sandwich. That sits in tension with the framework's headline ecological-dynamics / CLA branding, where perception and action are *coupled* and decisions emerge in the player–environment interaction rather than via a separate interpret/decide stage. The Venn (overlapping, not a strict pipeline) softens it, and the emphasis on varied, game-like practice is genuinely representative-design-flavoured — but the explicit decision model is IP at heart. This is a common inconsistency in applied "ecological" frameworks, and it's the point where the Swedish model and Finland's pelikäsitys (understand → read → decide) actually converge.

## Practice organisation

- Practice content is split into categories ("Me and the ball," "Me and the basket," small-sided games such as 2v2, 3v2, etc.), with a **recommended distribution of practice time** (a pie chart). From U8 up, total practice time should be divided roughly per that circle. Slices can be reordered and revisited within a session.
- **Playing time:** equal for everyone up to U12; from U13–U16 it may partly reflect practice attendance, but everyone dressed for a game should still get plenty of court time.
