### Item 6 in detail (the most important one)

The source is USA Volleyball, *Volleyball Systems & Strategies* (Human Kinetics, 2009), excerpt "Block Inside and Dig the Line Strategy", reproduced on Coaches Insider. It describes three hand settings for the outside blocker relative to "the point where the ball will cross the net":

1. **Inside arm at the crossing point.** The outside arm stays wider, protecting some of the line, and the middle closes with their outside hand to take more of the angle.
2. **Outside hand at the crossing point.** Seals the strong crosscourt hit (especially with the middle helping). The line and cut-back shots are left open.
3. **Head on the contact point** (one hand inside, one outside). Guards cross and line equally. A sensible default when hitter tendencies are unknown or in a one-on-one block.

Cathy George (Art of Coaching Volleyball, snippet only) agrees for a right-side blocker against an outside hitter: line up the **head** with the ball against a line hitter and the **(outside) hand** with the ball against a crosscourt hitter. Mike Diehl (volleyball1on1) adds that the hand on the line side presses toward the sideline so the blocker isn't tooled.

Applied to our court:

| Our blocker | Opponent attacker | Inside hand | Outside hand | Take line (close it) | Give line (open it) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Right side (zone 2) | Their left side (zone 4) | **Left** | Right | Left (inside) hand on the ball | Right (outside) hand on the ball |
| Left side (zone 4) | Their right side (zone 2) | Right | **Left** | Right (inside) hand on the ball | Left (outside) hand on the ball |

So the 2018 text, "left hand at the ball" both vs zone 4 with line closed and vs zone 2 with line open, is mechanically correct. It works only because "left" is the inside hand on one side and the outside hand on the other. Juniors will find that confusing. Recommend: *"sisäkäsi pallon kohdalle = linja kiinni; ulkokäsi pallon kohdalle = linja auki"* (Jarkko to author the Finnish; this is a gloss, not a translation).

# Indoor volleyball mechanics reference

Same rules as the beach section: each entry cites at least one external authoritative source. Never assert a mechanic here without a source.

---

## Outside blocker hand placement: line vs. angle

The outside (pin) blocker sets the block. The middle closes to them. Where the outside blocker puts their hands relative to the point where the ball will cross the net decides what is taken and what is given:

- **Inside hand on the ball:** the rest of the block sits outside the ball, so the line is protected. The middle closes with their outside hand to take more of the angle.
- **Outside hand on the ball:** the block sits inside the ball, so the crosscourt is sealed and the line (and cut-back) is open to the defence.
- **Head on the ball** (one hand each side): line and angle covered equally. The default when hitter tendencies are unknown or when blocking one-on-one.

Inside/outside are relative to the court. For our right-side (zone 2) blocker facing their left-side attacker, the inside hand is the **left** hand. For our left-side (zone 4) blocker facing their right-side attacker, the inside hand is the **right** hand. Write "inside hand" / "outside hand", never "left hand" / "right hand": the same physical hand means opposite things on the two sides.

The line-side hand presses toward the sideline (turned in) so a hard hit off it stays in court rather than tooling the block.

## Blocker and defender eye sequence

**Blockers:** ball – setter – ball – hitter (BSBH). Blockers have nothing between them and the hitter and very little time, so reading the setter early matters most.

**Back-court defenders** (Lebedew's variant): the block sits between them and the hitter, so the block enters the sequence. Single block: ball – blockers – ball – hitter. Multiple block: ball – blockers – ball – block.

## Defensive systems: names

- **Perimeter (6-back):** back-row wings around 20 ft (6 m) off the net near the sidelines. Middle back on the end line, centred. The off-blocker takes tips and short shots. The line digger stays deep on the line.
- **Rotation:** the back-row player behind the blocked side (right back vs. a left-side attack) moves up behind the block for tips. The middle back rotates toward the line.
- **6-up (rover / middle-up):** the middle back plays up and "roves behind the block" for tips and off-speed. Wings play deep, near the end line, about 5 ft in from the sideline.

Base distances are not standardised. One Human Kinetics base puts the side backs 15 ft (4.6 m) from the net and 7 ft (2.1 m) inside the sidelines, moving to about 20 ft (6.1 m) when the middle back plays up. Sources tie depth to the opponent's attack and the system. None found sets depth by gender.

## Serve receive (5-1)

The standard pattern is three passers: libero plus the two outside hitters. The setter, middles and usually the opposite are released to attack. Variations are legitimate: the opposite can pass in place of a weaker outside hitter, and USA Volleyball recommends designing receive for two, three or four passers.

Passing target: 4–5 ft (about 1.2–1.5 m) off the net. USA women's national team uses 4 ft. GMS recommends about 5 ft to cut overpasses and tight passes. The right accuracy demand varies by level.

## Free-ball transition

On "free", the setter releases to the target immediately. Front-row hitters leave the net to about the 3 m line so they can make a full approach. Back-court players pass.

## Attack coverage

The three players closest to the attacker cover close. The two furthest cover deep. Versus a left-side (zone 4) attack with a quick in front of the setter, the setter steps off the net to fill the gap and the left back covers behind the hitter. Right-side attack mirrors this, but because the setter has left right back, the middle back has to come across to be the third close cover.

## Indoor tips are legal

Indoors, an open-hand tip is legal as long as the ball is cleanly hit and not caught or thrown (FIVB Rule 13.1.2, with 9.2.2). This is the opposite of beach, where an open-hand finger tip is a fault (see "Legal vs. illegal soft attacks" above). In indoor content, "tip" is a normal attack option.
