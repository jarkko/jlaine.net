# Finnish Volleyball — Practice Design Guide

### Companion to the development model — how to design representative practice (with worked cases)

A working draft. This is the **"show, don't just tell" engine** for the development model (`finnish-volleyball-development-model-DRAFT.md`). It is **not a drill bank.** Its job is to make coaches *designers* of representative practice — not dispensers of recipes.

---

## 0. Read this first: why this is a design guide, not a drill list

A list of ready-made drills would quietly recreate the problem the model is trying to fix: coaches copying tasks they don't understand (the reproduction problem, §1 of the model). It also cuts against the whole approach — in ecological dynamics the coach's job is to **design environments attuned to *these* players in *this* gym**, not to run a fixed catalogue. And the real gap in coaching isn't "not enough drills," it's "no method for choosing and shaping tasks" (Kennedy & O'Brien 2024).

So the examples below are **worked design cases**, not prescriptions. Each one shows *the reasoning* and *how you'd change it*, and ends with a prompt to make it your own. **The goal is for coaches to outgrow these examples.** Two rules to keep us honest:

- **Numbers are ranges and starting points, never gospel.** Court sizes, scores, rep counts — all to be moved to fit your players. If a case reads like a recipe, you're using it wrong.
- **If a constraint isn't doing a job you can name, drop it.** Constraints are tools for a purpose, not decoration.

---

## 1. The design loop (the actual method)

Run this for any skill, age, or session. It's the thing to internalise; the cases in §3 are just it, worked through.

1. **Name the game problem / the read.** What does the player actually have to perceive and decide in the match? (e.g. "read whether the set lets me attack or I should free-ball it.") If you can't name a read, you're probably about to design a closed drill.
2. **Choose the most representative form that still works.** The default unit is a rally or segment with an opponent and the relevant information present — keep the net, keep the read. Make it *simpler*, **don't decompose it** — scale the *whole* skill (lighter/softer ball, lower net, smaller court, fewer players, slower feed) rather than breaking it into closed parts. This is **task simplification, not decomposition**, and it's the evidence-backed alternative to "teach the basics first" (Gray 2025, Ch. 7; Deuker 2023; Caldeira 2023 — see model §7). (Indoor caveat, §6a of the model: small-sided ≠ automatically representative — preserve net width, roles, the relevant cues.)
3. **Add one constraint that amplifies the right information.** Rule, space, scoring, equipment, or player numbers — chosen to make the key affordance stand out or to make a better solution reachable. *One* at a time, so you (and the player) can read its effect.
4. **Score it so the game does the teaching.** Scoring is how you make the long-run consequence local and immediate (cf. the "aggregate-cost" fieldstone). Reward the behaviour you want to emerge — without telling the player how to move.
5. **Watch, then regress or progress.** Are players succeeding ~⅔ of the time and exploring? Too hard → make it easier / invite the affordance. Too easy or stale → add variability or pressure. Adjust the constraint, not the player's technique.
6. **Let them reflect, briefly.** One question that points attention at the read ("which serves were hardest to handle — what did you do differently?"). Then play again.

The four reasons we ever add a constraint (after Trinsey/CLA): **destabilise** an over-grooved solution, **encourage exploration**, **amplify information / invite an affordance** (sometimes by making it easier), **give transition feedback** about whether the search is working.

## 2. The smell test (diagnostics for any task — including your own)

Hold any task — a case below, or one you invented — against these. They're the §8 golden principles used as on-the-spot checks:

- **Is there a read?** Something real to perceive (server, set, block, defender, net, wind)? If the information is removed, it isn't representative.
- **Many *and varied* contacts?** Volume is fine — sameness isn't. Is every rep a bit different?
- **Right level of representativeness for the age/code?** Right ball, net, court, information. (Scaled equipment is a tool, not dumbing-down.)
- **Are we judging the solution, not the form?** Did it solve the rally problem — rather than "were the arms textbook"?
- **Is the chain intact where it matters?** reception→set→attack practised coupled, not as separate stations.
- **Is each constraint earning its place?** Name its job, or cut it.

If a task fails these, change it — that's the skill we're building in coaches.

## 3. Worked design cases

Each case lists: **code** (indoor / beach / both), a rough **stage**, the **read**, a **starting setup**, the **constraint + why**, **what to watch**, **regress / progress**, and a **make-it-yours** prompt. Treat the specifics as one instance of the design loop, not as "the drill."

### Case A — Serve as exploration: the two-serve game
- **Code:** both · **Stage:** Learn-to-Train and up (≈U13+)
- **Read:** risk/reward of target and aggression against a real receiving side.
- **Setup:** server gets **two** serves per turn; play out the rally from whichever lands. Target zones on the far court.
- **Constraint + why:** the second serve removes the single-serve fear, which *invites exploration* on the first — players try the aggressive serve they'd never risk under one-and-done. (Grounded in the 2024 serve-exploration study: a 2-serve game produced **faster, more aggressive first serves** — ~60 vs ~56 km/h, more jump-topspin attempts — because ~80% of players reported the second serve cut their fear/pressure. Honest design note: accuracy *dropped* and movement variability didn't change — the value is the **exploration it unlocks**, not an instant accuracy gain.)
- **Watch:** are first serves actually more adventurous than in normal play? Are players trying serves they'd normally avoid?
- **Regress / progress:** regress — bigger target zones, shorter distance, lower net. Progress — one-serve "pressure" rounds interleaved; zone values that reward attacking the seams.
- **Make it yours:** what would make *your* servers explore more — the scoring, the targets, or who's receiving? Change one and watch.

### Case B — Earn the attack (the reception→set→attack chain)
- **Code:** both (beach: 2v2 natural; indoor: keep net width + a real serve) · **Stage:** ≈U12+
- **Read:** the incoming serve, then "is this pass good enough to attack, or do I keep the rally alive?"
- **Setup:** live serve into a receiving group; they pass→set→attempt to attack/tip over. **Score:** 1 for a controlled pass, **2 if the pass was good enough to attack off it.**
- **Constraint + why:** the 2-point bonus *steers attention* to a passable, attackable ball — without the coach saying "pass high to the middle." The chain stays whole, so the pass *means something*.
- **Watch:** are receivers adjusting to passes that feed an attack, not just any pass up?
- **Regress / progress:** regress — underhand serve, lower net, allow a catch-and-set for the youngest. Progress — add a block/defender to read; vary serve type.
- **Make it yours:** what's the smallest change that keeps the chain but fits your group's level — the serve, the net, or the scoring?

### Case C — Read the set (block ↔ attacker, the across-the-net coupling)
- **Code:** indoor (blocking) + beach (block-or-pull) · **Stage:** ≈U15+
- **Read:** blocker reads pass quality + setter + hitter to commit; attacker reads the block.
- **Setup:** attacking side runs a real (varied) set; blocker(s) must read and act. Score blocker touches/stuffs vs attacker kills.
- **Constraint + why:** restrict the pass to around the 3 m line and take away the middle option → the blocker gets a clean front/back read and starts moving dynamically (Trinsey). You've *amplified the information* by removing noise, not by scripting the block.
- **Watch:** is the blocker reading and committing, or guessing early?
- **Regress / progress:** regress — slower tempo, fewer attack options. Progress — reintroduce the middle; add transition off the block.
- **Beach variant:** blocker chooses to **block or pull (peel)**; rule: "if you pull, defend the shot you just showed." Now the read drives a whole-body decision, which is the beach game.
- **Make it yours:** what's the one option you can remove so the read becomes clean for *your* blockers?

### Case D — First ball to counter (defence → transition)
- **Code:** both (beach: 2v2 is the real thing) · **Stage:** ≈U14+
- **Read:** the attacker's approach/shot, then dig→set→counter.
- **Setup:** a ball is attacked into the defending side; they must dig, set, and **counterattack**. Wash scoring; **2 for a counter-kill.**
- **Constraint + why:** requiring the completed transition to win the point makes the *whole chain* the unit, not an isolated dig. The bonus makes converting defence to offence the salient goal.
- **Watch:** do defenders already move toward their transition role as they dig?
- **Regress / progress:** regress — coach-controlled entry ball, generous court. Progress — live attack entry; shrink recovery time; add a block.
- **Make it yours:** how hard should the entry ball be so your players succeed about two-thirds of the time?

### Case E — Set the advantage (setter decision)
- **Code:** indoor mainly · **Stage:** ≈U15+
- **Read:** which hitter is advantaged (vs block/defence) on this pass?
- **Setup:** setter gets varied passes; two hitters available; manipulate the other side so one hitter is favoured. Score more when the ball goes to the open/advantaged hitter.
- **Constraint + why:** the manufactured advantage *creates a real read*; the scoring rewards perceiving and deciding, not running a pre-called set.
- **Watch:** is the setter perceiving the situation, or delivering a habit?
- **Regress / progress:** regress — make the advantage obvious and static. Progress — make it subtle and shifting; add a clock.
- **Avoid:** pre-calling the set — it deletes the read.
- **Make it yours:** what's the clearest way to make "the open hitter" change from rep to rep in your gym?

### Case F — Taming the ball (the youngest)
- **Code:** both · **Stage:** earliest (≈U9–U11) — this *replaces* closed line drills here
- **Read:** how the ball behaves — built through varied, playful contact.
- **Setup:** playful ball-mastery — wall rallies, partner keep-it-up with any body part, pokey-only pepper, underhand slaps, self-sets with backspin, "keep it alive any way you can." Score = streaks and personal/pair challenges.
- **Constraint + why:** *unconventional contacts are encouraged*, not corrected — the aim is a relationship with the ball and thousands of varied touches before any "correct" technique is imposed (see the ball-taming fieldstone). Volleyball's no-rest rule is handled with a wall, a partner, or self-feeds.
- **Watch:** are kids exploring and having fun with the ball, or waiting in a line?
- **Regress / progress:** regress — bigger/softer/lighter ball, catch allowed. Progress — add a target, a partner, a mini-rally.
- **Make it yours:** which playful challenge gets *your* group taking the most varied contacts per minute?

## 4. The trap to avoid (say it out loud)

The failure mode for this whole guide is coaches treating §3 as drills to run verbatim — "constraints as a bag of tricks." Guard against it explicitly: in coach education, always teach the **loop (§1)** and the **smell test (§2)** first, and present every case as "here's the thinking — now change it." A coach who can *diagnose and adjust* any task has the skill; a coach who can only run our six examples does not.
